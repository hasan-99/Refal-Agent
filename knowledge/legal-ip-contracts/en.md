## Registering a trademark in Cyprus and across the EU
<!-- facts: MB-F63 -->
Trademark registration is handled on two levels, and the choice between them is a commercial decision before it is a legal one.

**Cyprus national registration.** The mark is registered in Cyprus and protected in Cyprus. It suits a business whose market, customers and trading activity stay on the island.

**EU registration.** A single filing covers every EU member state at once. It suits a business selling across Europe, shipping across borders, or building a brand it intends to license or expand.

Two points decide most files. Protection is granted per class of goods and services, so the classes have to match what the business actually sells now and plans to sell next. And a mark has to be available, which means a search before a filing rather than after a refusal.

The registration covers the brand as it is used in trade: the name, and depending on the filing, the logo and the stylised form. What it does not do is protect an idea, a business model, or a name nobody uses.

## The company name and the brand are two different registrations
<!-- facts: MB-F63 -->
This is the most common misunderstanding in the whole area, and it is expensive when it surfaces late.

Registering a company at the Registrar reserves a company name for corporate purposes. It establishes a legal entity. It does not give exclusive rights over that name as a brand in the market, and it does not stop another business from using a similar name commercially.

A trademark does the opposite job. It protects the sign the customers recognise, in the classes it is registered for, in the territory it is registered in. It is the asset you enforce against copying, and it is the asset a buyer or an investor looks for when brand value is part of the deal.

Two consequences follow. A company can exist for years with no brand protection at all. And a brand can be protected by someone else even though your company name was registered first. Both are normal, and both are checked at the start rather than discovered during a dispute.

## Shareholders Agreements, the rules between the owners
<!-- facts: MB-F64 -->
A Shareholders Agreement, often called an SHA, is the governance agreement between the owners and partners of a company. Its purpose is to settle how decisions are made while everyone still agrees, so that future disputes have a written answer instead of a standoff.

It is the document that typically deals with how decisions get taken and which ones need more than a simple majority, what happens if a shareholder wants to sell or exit, what happens if one of them stops contributing, how new partners or investors are admitted, and how a deadlock between owners is broken.

The articles of association of the company are not a substitute. The articles are the public constitutional document and are deliberately general. The SHA is the private agreement that reflects what the partners actually agreed between themselves.

The time to put one in place is at the start, when the relationship is good and the terms are easy to discuss. Drafting one after the disagreement has already happened is a different and much harder exercise.

## Service and employment agreements
<!-- facts: MB-F65 -->
The contracts a trading company runs on fall into a few recognisable types, and each is drafted for the relationship it covers.

**Service agreements.** What is delivered, to what standard, on what timetable, for what fee, and who owns the result. The ownership clause matters more than people expect, particularly where the work produces software, designs or content.

**Development agreements.** Scope, milestones, acceptance, change requests, and the transfer of intellectual property in what is built. Without an explicit transfer, the developer can retain rights in work the client believes it bought.

**Employment contracts.** The terms between the company and its staff: role, pay, hours, notice, confidentiality, and the treatment of anything the employee creates in the course of their work.

A company that trades on verbal agreements and copied templates is not saving anything. It is deferring a cost, and the cost arrives at the worst possible moment: a client who refuses to pay, a developer who claims the code, or an employee who leaves with the customer list.

## Website terms and GDPR advisory
<!-- facts: MB-F66 -->
Any company with a website, an app, a shop or a customer list has two jobs here.

**Terms and conditions.** The rules of using the site or the service, and where there is a shop, the terms of sale: ordering, payment, delivery, cancellation, refunds, liability and the applicable law. This is the document that decides what the business is actually promising, and it is the one that gets read when something goes wrong.

**GDPR advisory.** European data protection compliance for a business handling personal data of people in the EU. In practice it covers the privacy notice that explains what is collected and why, the lawful basis for the processing, how consent is handled where consent is the basis, data subject rights, the agreements needed with processors such as hosting and marketing providers, and what happens in the event of a breach.

This applies to the small operation as well as the large one. The obligation follows the handling of personal data, not the size of the company.

## What the legal team needs to start
<!-- kind: discovery -->
A short set of answers turns a general question into a concrete piece of work.

**For a brand.** The exact name and whether a logo is involved, what the business sells, and which markets matter: Cyprus only, the EU, or beyond.

**For an SHA.** Who the owners are, the shareholding split, who works in the business and who only invests, and whether anyone is joining or exiting soon.

**For contracts.** What type of relationship it is, whether anything is already signed, and whether the work produces intellectual property that has to move.

**For GDPR.** What personal data the business collects, where the customers are, and which third party tools are in use.

With those answers the legal team can scope the work rather than describe it.

## Where this description stops
<!-- kind: boundary -->
This is a description of a service portfolio, not legal advice on anyone's situation. It is general information about what these instruments are and what they do.

Drafting and filing are done by the legal team on the specific facts. A trademark is filed after availability and classes are checked, an SHA is drafted on the real shareholding and the real intentions of the partners, and a contract is drafted or reviewed against the actual document and the actual relationship. None of that can be done from a general description.

No view is given here on whether a particular contract is valid, binding or enforceable in your situation, and no position is taken on an existing dispute. Those are questions about a document and a set of facts, and they are answered after both have been read.

Rights outside this portfolio, such as patents, industrial designs and copyright in software and content, are assessed by the legal team case by case. The tax treatment of income from intellectual property is a separate subject handled on the tax side.

Official filing fees are set by the registries and are confirmed live rather than quoted from a document, because they are third party charges and they change.
