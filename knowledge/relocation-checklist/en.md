## Schools: public, private and international
<!-- facts: MB-F42 -->
Three school types exist side by side in Cyprus, and families usually compare them before anything else.

**Public schools.** State funded and free to attend. Teaching follows the national curriculum and is mainly in Greek, so they suit families who plan to settle long term and want the children to grow up in the local language.

**Private schools.** Fee paying, with smaller classes and a wider choice of subjects and languages. Programmes vary from school to school.

**International schools.** Fee paying, most of them teaching a British or English language curriculum, which is the usual route to IGCSE and A Level. Families who may move again later, or who want continuity with an English language system, normally look here first.

Places are applied for school by school. Availability, the admission process and the point in the school year when a child joins all depend on the individual school rather than on one central rule, which is why the shortlist is built around the ages of the children and the area you expect to live in.

## Healthcare: GESY and private insurance
<!-- facts: MB-F43 -->
Cyprus runs a national health system called GESY. It is the public framework through which registered beneficiaries access personal doctors, specialists, laboratory tests, medicines and hospital care, with each beneficiary registering with a personal doctor as the entry point to the system.

Alongside it, many relocating families add private health insurance. Private cover is used for faster access to specific specialists and private hospitals, for services a policy covers in a broader way, and for the period before a new arrival is settled into the public system.

The two are not alternatives in practice. The common pattern is GESY as the base layer and a private policy on top of it, sized to the family.

Whether you qualify for GESY, and from what point, depends on your residence status and your registration, so that is confirmed against your own permit route rather than assumed.

## Housing, daily costs and buying a car
<!-- kind: boundary; facts: MB-F44 -->
Housing and transport are the two costs that vary most, and this is exactly where generic numbers mislead.

Rent depends on the city, the neighbourhood, the size and condition of the property, and whether it comes furnished. A family of five and a couple with no children are not shopping in the same market at all. So the figure that is worth having is a fresh current estimate built on your city and your family size, not an average copied from an article.

Buying a car works the same way. The variables that move the cost are whether you buy new or used, whether you buy locally or import, registration and road tax, and insurance. Each of those shifts with the specific vehicle, its age and its engine.

Government and third party fees are quoted from the current fee list at the time of the enquiry, for the same reason. They are revised, and a number repeated from memory is the one that turns out to be wrong.

What can be done here is to set out the cost headings you need to plan for. Current figures are put against those headings once the city and the household are known.

## What actually decides the city
<!-- kind: fact -->
The city question is usually answered by four practical constraints rather than by a ranking.

**Where the school is.** Once a school is chosen, the realistic neighbourhoods are the ones within a sane daily drive of it. For most families the school decides the area, not the other way round.

**Where the work or the business sits.** A commute that looks fine on a map is a different thing twice a day in summer traffic.

**The type of home you want.** An apartment near the centre, a house with a garden, or something near the sea are different budgets and different areas.

**Language and community.** The international school sector teaches in English, and the cities differ in how large and how international their resident communities are. If an Arabic speaking community, places of worship, or Arabic language services matter to you, say so early. That narrows the city and the neighbourhood faster than budget does.

Which city comes out ahead is a result of those four for your family, so it is worth answering them before comparing locations.

## The first months after arrival
<!-- kind: example -->
A relocation file tends to run as several parallel tracks rather than one queue.

**Residence first.** The permit route governs most of what follows, including registration with the health system and what the family members may do.

**Housing is usually rented before it is bought.** Families who rent for the first period get to test the area and the school run before committing to a purchase.

**School enrolment runs on the school calendar.** Applications, assessments and available places follow each school's own timetable, so the earlier the shortlist exists, the more choice there is.

**Healthcare and a car come next.** Registration for GESY, a decision on private insurance, and transport for the daily routine.

Running them in parallel is what shortens the settling in period. Doing them strictly one after another is what stretches it.

## What we need to know to build your checklist
<!-- kind: discovery; facts: MB-F42 -->
The first question is always the same one, because it unlocks the rest.

How old are the children? Ages decide which school options are realistic, which curricula are still a sensible switch, and therefore which areas are suitable.

After that, four short answers finish the picture.

Who is relocating, and when? Full family at once, or one person first with the family following.

What is the residence route you are on or considering?

Do you need an English language curriculum, or is the local system an option for you?

Do you expect to rent first or to buy straight away?

With those, the checklist stops being a general list and becomes a sequence for your household, with a current cost estimate attached to the headings that actually apply to you.
