## Three different structures, not one scale
<!-- facts: MB-J3 -->
Cyprus, Malta and Bulgaria are all European Union member states, and real businesses operate out of all three. What sits behind the headline numbers is not the same thing in each.

**Bulgaria** publishes a 10% corporate tax rate, the lowest headline figure of the three. Next to it sit practical constraints: banking is harder to arrange and to keep, and the language and the operating environment add friction for a founder who does not work in Bulgarian.

**Malta** runs a refund system. Tax is paid at company level and part of it is refunded afterwards at shareholder level. It functions, and it is administratively complex: the mechanics, the timing and the shareholder reporting all have to be handled correctly for the intended result to appear.

**Cyprus** applies a direct system. Corporate tax starts at 15% from 2026, applied at company level with no refund cycle to administer. English is widely used in business and administration, there is an active property market, and residency is a stable, documented route.

## Why the rate is only one element of the comparison
<!-- facts: MB-J3 -->
A tax rate is one line inside a structure. Four other things usually decide more than it does.

**Where your customers are.** Selling into the EU, outside it, or both, changes the VAT position and the invoicing long before it changes the tax bill.

**Banking.** A company that cannot open or keep an account cannot trade, whatever its rate is. This is the most common reason a cheap structure turns expensive.

**The activity.** Trading goods, selling services, software and intellectual property, and holding all behave differently under the same headline rate.

**Residency and housing.** If you or the family intend to live there, language, schools and the property market stop being details.

A headline rate is also not the operating cost. Accounting, audit, substance and compliance are paid in all three, and those are usually the numbers a founder feels first.

## What we will not claim
<!-- kind: boundary; facts: MB-J0 -->
We do not attack other countries and we do not claim Cyprus is always the best answer. Bulgaria's rate is real. Malta's system delivers for the companies built around it. For some businesses either one is the better fit.

The right jurisdiction depends on the specific business. Without knowing where the customers are, how the money is banked, what the activity is, and whether residency and housing matter, a comparison is not a real comparison.

So some things are stated here and some are deliberately not. We state the Cyprus position because it is ours to state. We do not publish another country's residency programmes, bank acceptance criteria or current cost levels; those are set by that country's authorities and institutions, they change, and they belong to a local specialist there. Nothing here is a guarantee about any application, any bank or any tax outcome.

## What we need from you to compare properly
<!-- kind: discovery; facts: MB-J3 -->
Four answers, and the comparison stops being generic.

Where are your customers and where is the money received?

What does the banking need to look like? Incoming payment methods, currencies, and whether a payment provider or a gateway is part of the picture.

What is the activity, in one sentence?

Do residency and housing matter to you or to the family, now or later?

With those, the question stops being which country prints the lowest rate and becomes which structure survives your actual operation. The tax detail then goes to a tax specialist, and the banking question goes to the institution that will decide it.
