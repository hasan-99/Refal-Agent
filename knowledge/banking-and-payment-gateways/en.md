## How opening a corporate bank account actually works
<!-- kind: fact -->
Opening an account is a separate process from incorporation. The company exists the moment the Registrar issues its certificates. It can only move money once an institution has onboarded it.

The usual sequence:

1. The company is incorporated and the corporate documents are issued.
2. The file is prepared: corporate documents, the ownership chain up to the real owners, a precise activity description, and the evidence behind it.
3. An application goes to the institution that fits the activity. That may be a bank, an Electronic Money Institution, or a payment provider.
4. The institution runs its own onboarding review and comes back with questions.
5. Questions are answered with documents rather than explanations.
6. The institution issues its decision and, where it is positive, activates the account.

Opening remotely is possible with some institutions and not with others. Several banks still ask for one in person meeting, while most Electronic Money Institutions onboard fully online. Which route is open to you depends on your activity, your residence, and the institution's policy at the time you apply.

## What KYC and AML actually mean for your file
<!-- kind: fact -->
KYC means Know Your Customer. AML means Anti-Money Laundering. Together they are the compliance framework every regulated institution in the EU applies before it accepts a client, and they are the reason the questions get detailed.

In practice the reviewer is answering four things:

- Who is really behind this company? Every shareholder and every ultimate beneficial owner, up the chain until a natural person is identified.
- What does the business actually do? Not the object clause in the constitutional documents, the real activity: what is sold, to whom, and how it gets paid for.
- Where does the money come from and where does it go? Expected volumes, expected counterparties, expected countries.
- Does the picture hold together? The activity, the documents, the website, the contracts and the projected turnover all have to describe the same business.

None of this is personal suspicion. It is the standard regulatory step every applicant goes through. A file that answers these four questions before they are asked spends far less time in review than one that answers them in instalments.

## The documents and information usually requested
<!-- kind: fact -->
Lists differ by institution, but the core of the file is predictable.

**Corporate documents.** Certificate of incorporation, certificate of directors and secretary, certificate of shareholders, certificate of registered office, and the memorandum and articles. Where the shareholder is another company, the same set for that entity plus a structure chart.

**Identity documents** for every director, shareholder and ultimate beneficial owner: a valid passport, a recent proof of residential address, and in many cases a bank reference or a professional reference.

**Business evidence.** A written description of the activity, the main suppliers and customers, sample invoices or contracts, a live website or marketplace storefront, and expected monthly turnover with the countries involved.

**Source of funds and source of wealth.** Where the money funding the company comes from, and how the owner's wealth was built over time. For a payment gateway the equivalent is the trading history of your existing shop or platform.

Documents in another language usually need a certified translation, and documents issued abroad often need an apostille. Gathering these before the application rather than during it is what keeps a review from reopening three times.

## Why your activity and your customer geography matter
<!-- kind: fact -->
Institutions do not treat all activities the same way. Each one works to an internal risk appetite, and some sectors sit outside it regardless of how well an individual file is built.

Activity matters because it predicts the payment pattern. A consulting company invoicing three EU clients a month looks nothing like a high volume e-commerce shop with card refunds and chargebacks, and neither looks like a business in crypto assets, gambling, adult content, arms, precious metals, or unlicensed financial services. Those last categories are restricted or excluded at most institutions and at most gateways.

Geography matters twice over. The countries your customers pay from and the countries your suppliers sit in both enter the assessment. Flows involving sanctioned jurisdictions or countries under enhanced monitoring trigger additional checks, whatever the company itself looks like.

This is also why the same company can be onboarded by one institution and declined by another in the same month. They are applying different internal policies to the same set of facts.

## What makes a file weak
<!-- kind: example -->
Files that stall or get declined tend to show the same handful of weaknesses.

- The activity description is vague. "Consulting" or "general trading" tells a reviewer nothing and invites a longer questionnaire.
- The story does not match itself. The website sells software, the invoices are for logistics, and the projected turnover fits neither.
- The ownership chain stops short. A corporate shareholder with no documents behind it leaves the ultimate beneficial owner unidentified.
- There is no evidence of real business. No contracts, no invoices, no platform history, no customers that can be named.
- Nothing explains the connection to Cyprus. No substance, no identifiable market, no commercial logic for the jurisdiction.
- Source of funds is asserted rather than documented.
- Answers arrive late and in fragments, so the review reopens again and again.

Every item on that list can be dealt with before an application is submitted. That is precisely why the file is built first and sent second.

## Banks and payment gateways are separate processes
<!-- kind: fact -->
A bank account and a payment gateway are two different applications, to two different institutions, under two different sets of criteria. Holding one does not produce the other.

A bank or an Electronic Money Institution gives the company an IBAN. It assesses the company, its owners, and the flow of funds through the account.

A payment gateway such as Stripe, PayPal, Amazon or Shopify gives the company the ability to accept card payments from end customers. It assesses the merchant: the website, the product, the delivery and refund policy, the terms of service, the chargeback exposure, and whether the legal entity and the trading name line up. The gateway also needs a bank account to settle into, which is why the order normally runs company, then banking, then gateway.

Each platform publishes its own list of restricted and prohibited businesses, and each runs its own review on its own schedule under the rules of the country where the account is registered. An outcome at one of them says nothing about the next.

## Who decides, and what cannot be committed to
<!-- kind: boundary; facts: MB-F28, MB-F29 -->
One rule here never bends. No definitive commitment can be given that a bank account will be opened or that a payment gateway will be activated, and that includes Stripe, PayPal, Amazon and Shopify. Anyone offering you that assurance is describing a decision that is not theirs to make.

The final decision belongs to the financial institution. It rests on that institution's own risk assessment and on its KYC and AML compliance requirements, applied by its own compliance team under its own regulator. We are not a party to that review. We cannot overrule it, accelerate it, or predict its outcome, and we will not pretend otherwise.

What is genuinely within our control is the quality of what reaches the reviewer. A company built correctly from day one, with the right documents, a coherent business story, and a structure that matches the actual activity, is a materially different file from one assembled after the fact once problems have already surfaced. That difference is real and worth stating plainly, which is why we state it instead of dressing it up as a certainty nobody can give.

## Timelines, ongoing monitoring, and the limits of our role
<!-- kind: boundary; facts: MB-F28 -->
Timelines belong to the institution as well. A review moves with the compliance team's workload, the complexity of the ownership structure, the season, and how quickly follow up questions are answered. We attach no date to a decision that is not ours, and a duration quoted by anyone outside the institution is an estimate rather than a commitment.

Two further limits are worth stating in the open. First, an outcome at one institution is not a pattern. Policies change, sometimes without notice, and a route that was open last quarter may be closed this one. Second, an account that opens stays under ongoing monitoring. Institutions re-examine existing clients, request updated documents, and can restrict or close an account later if the activity drifts from what was declared at onboarding.

What you can hold us to is the process: a complete and honest file, the right type of institution for your activity, and prompt handling of whatever the reviewer comes back with.
