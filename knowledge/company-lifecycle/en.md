## The ten stages of a Cyprus company
<!-- facts: MB-F1 -->
A Cyprus company moves through ten recognisable stages. Knowing them early makes the whole file easier to plan.

1. **Idea.** You decide what the company is for: trading, holding, software, property, or a group structure.
2. **Incorporation.** The name is reserved, the constitutional documents are filed, and the Registrar issues the certificates.
3. **Banking.** A bank account or a payment institution account is opened so the company can receive and send money.
4. **Tax.** The company is registered with the Tax Department and, where the activity requires it, for VAT.
5. **Accounting.** Books are kept from the first transaction, not at year end.
6. **Operations.** The company trades, invoices, contracts, and employs.
7. **Growth.** New markets, new entities, new shareholders, or new funding.
8. **Changes.** Directors, shareholders, share capital, name, or registered address are amended as the business evolves.
9. **Renewal.** The annual obligations repeat: levy where applicable, accounts, audit, tax return.
10. **Closure.** If the company is no longer needed, it is formally wound up.

Most enquiries arrive at stage one or two. The stages after that are where the real cost and the real planning sit, which is why we map them at the start.

## How long the early stages actually take
<!-- facts: MB-F2 -->
Incorporation is the stage everyone times first, and it is the one with a usual answer. Taken through the full official procedures, a Cyprus Ltd is normally registered in about two weeks, counted from the point where your documents are complete and signed.

So the clock starts later than most people expect. Name approval, identity documents and signatures all sit in front of those two weeks. A file that arrives complete moves at a very different pace from one that is still collecting passports.

The stages after incorporation keep their own rhythm. The bank approves its own account, and tax registration follows the authority's queue, so neither of those is promised here.

One thing is worth settling before you begin, because it decides how fast that clock starts: who signs what. The documents that carry your name are signed by you. Our team prepares them, files them and chases the Registrar, and nobody signs on your behalf without a separate written authority.

## The difference between reserving a name and actual registration
<!-- facts: MB-F1, MB-F2 -->
These two steps get collapsed into one constantly, and the difference between them is worth knowing before you start.

Reserving a name means the name is filed with the Registrar and held for you. That is all it does. The company does not exist yet, it has no certificates, and it cannot open an account or sign a contract.

Actual registration is the filing that creates the legal entity. The constitutional documents go in, the Registrar reviews them, and the certificates come out at the end. Only once that is done is the company registered and able to trade.

So a reserved name is a start, not a finish. Registering the company is what the usual timeline is counted against, and a reservation sitting next to an incomplete file moves nothing along.

## Where the stages usually surprise people
<!-- kind: example; facts: MB-F1 -->
Three stages are the ones clients most often did not plan for.

**Banking** is a separate process from incorporation. The company exists the moment the Registrar issues its certificates, but it cannot transact until an account is live, and that account is approved by the institution, not by us.

**Accounting** starts immediately. A company that trades for eight months and only then looks for a bookkeeper has eight months of reconstruction to pay for.

**Changes** are normal. Bringing in an investor, adding a director, or moving the registered address are routine filings. They are not a sign that the original structure was wrong.

If you tell us which stage you are at right now, the answer stops being generic and starts being about your file.

## Closure is a procedure, not a decision to stop
<!-- facts: MB-F18 -->
Stage ten deserves a straight answer, because it is the stage people assume takes care of itself. Closing a Cyprus company follows formal legal liquidation procedures, and simply neglecting the company is not a way of closing it.

A company left alone stays on the register. Its filing obligations continue and the responsibilities of its directors continue with them, so an abandoned file normally costs more to tidy up later than a planned closure costs now.

That makes the answer to "can I close it later" a yes with a shape to it. Closing is straightforward in the sense that matters: there is a defined route, it has its own paperwork and its own timeline, and it is planned rather than improvised. If what you actually want is to pause the company instead of closing it, that is a different route and we map it separately.

## What the life cycle does not tell you
<!-- kind: boundary -->
The life cycle is a map of the stages, not a quotation and not a decision.

It does not tell you which structure suits you. A trading company, a holding company, a branch, and a subsidiary all follow the same ten stages and have very different consequences.

It does not promise a timeline for any stage that depends on a third party. Bank onboarding and government processing times belong to the bank and to the authorities.

It does not replace advice on your personal position. Where you are tax resident, where your customers are, and what you already own all change the answer, and those are questions for a specialist rather than a general description.

Most people arrive at the same point: they want to incorporate and they don't know where to start. Three short answers turn the general map into your plan.

What is the activity? Trading goods, selling services, holding shares or property, or software and intellectual property.

Where are the customers and the money? Inside the EU, outside it, or both. Founders living abroad are the normal case here rather than the exception.

What already exists? A company elsewhere, a partner, an investor arriving later, or a clean start from scratch.

With those three, we can say which stages matter most for you, which ones are quick, and where the real planning time goes.
