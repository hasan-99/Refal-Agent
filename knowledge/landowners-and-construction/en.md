## Why a land file and a tender file are not ordinary property enquiries
<!-- facts: MB-F56 -->
A landowner partnership deal and a major construction tender are assets worth millions. They sit in a completely different class from a buyer looking for an apartment.

The failure mode is well known. These enquiries arrive worded like any other property message, get answered like any other property message, and the opportunity is gone before anyone senior ever sees it. Treating a land development partnership as an ordinary property enquiry is how large deals are lost.

So these files are filtered quickly and quietly. Quickly, because the window on them is short and the owner is usually talking to more than one group. Quietly, because the filtering is a few precise questions rather than an interrogation, and because nothing commercial is discussed before the right people are looking at the file.

The purpose of the filter is to protect two things at once: the executive team's time, and the serious commercial counterparty who should not be handled by a general enquiry flow.

## What a landowner opportunity sounds like
<!-- facts: MB-F57 -->
The signal is simple and it is almost always in the customer's first message.

The customer says they own land in Cyprus and want to develop it, or that they are looking to partner with a developer. Any version of that phrasing is the trigger: I have a plot and I want to build on it, I own land and I am looking for a development partner, I want to do a joint development on my land.

The distinction that matters: this person is not buying. They already hold the asset. What they are looking for is a route to turn land into a built project, either by developing it themselves with the right partner or by contributing it into a partnership.

In a landowner partnership, the land is brought into a development rather than sold outright, and the commercial terms between the owner and the developer are set per file. There is no standard template, which is exactly why the file goes to people who structure these for a living.

## The four questions on a land file
<!-- kind: discovery; facts: MB-F58 -->
Four answers make a land file reviewable. Without them it is a conversation; with them it is a proposal.

**Where is the land?** The city, the area and the specific location. Location decides what the plot is worth to a developer more than any other single input.

**How big is it?** The area of the plot.

**What is the building density?** The permitted density, meaning how much buildable area the plot carries. Two plots of identical size in the same town can be worth very different things on density alone, and this is the number the development side asks for first.

**Do preliminary permits already exist?** Whether any planning or building permits have been obtained, applied for, or never started. A plot that already carries permits is a different proposition from raw land, and it changes who is interested and how fast they move.

Partial answers are still useful. Even the location and the density move a file forward.

## Where a land file goes next
<!-- facts: MB-F59 -->
Once those answers exist, the file leaves the ordinary property track.

The reason is stated plainly to the owner: because this concerns a land development opportunity and not an ordinary property purchase, the file requires direct review by our development and investment team.

That is not a holding message. It is a routing decision with a real consequence. The development and investment team is the function that assesses development potential, structures partnership arrangements, and decides what the group is prepared to do on a specific plot. Nobody else is positioned to answer those questions, and nobody else should be answering them.

What happens next is a direct review of the file rather than a generic reply, which is also why the four questions are asked up front. The team reviews a file, not a message thread.

## What a construction tender enquiry sounds like
<!-- facts: MB-F60 -->
The second high value signal is construction rather than land.

The customer says they need a contractor for a large project, or that they have a construction tender. Variants of the same thing: we are tendering a project, we are looking for a main contractor, we have a build and we need someone to price it.

This is a contracting opportunity, not a sales enquiry. The counterparty is usually a company, a developer, an institution or a public body, and they are typically running a process with a deadline rather than browsing.

It is treated with the same urgency as a landowner file and for the same reason: the value at stake is in a different class from an ordinary property enquiry, and a slow or generic response removes the group from the process before it has started.

## The four questions on a tender file
<!-- kind: discovery; facts: MB-F61 -->
Four answers again, and they are the ones the construction side needs before it can look at anything.

**How big is the project?** The scale of the build, in whatever terms the customer thinks in: number of units, floors, scope of works, or the overall size of the development.

**Where is it?** The location of the project. Access, region and site conditions all follow from this.

**Do engineering plans exist?** Specifically the BOQ and the architectural plans. A tender with a BOQ and drawings can be assessed; a tender without them is still at the concept stage and is a different conversation.

**When is the planned start?** The intended timeline for beginning works, and any fixed dates the project is committed to.

These four turn an enquiry into an executive summary that the construction arm can act on.

## What is never quoted before the construction arm reviews
<!-- kind: boundary; facts: MB-F62 -->
A construction tender is escalated as an urgent executive summary to the group's construction arm. That is the whole action, and it is deliberately the whole action.

No prices and no preliminary estimates are given by the agent at any point. Not a rate, not a range, not a rough idea, not an indication, and not a figure offered as a friendly starting point. There is no acceptable version of a number here.

The reason is commercial, not procedural. A casual figure becomes the anchor for every later negotiation, and an anchor set by someone without the costing data in front of them damages the position of the side it was meant to help. On a project of this size, one careless number can cost more than the enquiry was worth.

Pricing on a tender is produced by the construction arm from the BOQ, the drawings, the site and the programme. Until that work is done, the honest and correct answer is that the file is with them and the figures come from them.

## What cannot be answered in advance
<!-- kind: boundary -->
Permits are decided by the planning and building authorities on the specific plot, its zone and its density. Which permits a development needs, and how long they take, is their determination and their timetable, not ours. No permit outcome can be guaranteed by anyone, and none is predicted here. That is also why the file asks early whether preliminary permits already exist: it is a fact about the plot, not a forecast about it.

How the development is taxed, and whether a Cyprus company is the right vehicle for it, are structuring questions. They are answered by the corporate and tax specialists on the actual facts of the file once it is open, and not from a general description.

Whether the group wants to take part in a specific development or tender is a commercial decision made after review. Interest at the enquiry stage is not a commitment in either direction.
